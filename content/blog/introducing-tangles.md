We&rsquo;re going to do something different today; we&rsquo;re going to announce our intention to build a product before we&rsquo;ve even started building the product. In the spirit of the lean methodology, we&rsquo;re going to talk a bit about the void we see, how we want to fill it, and the principles we want to carry through the project.

<!--more-->

It has been nearly 16 years since the word &#34;blog&#34; was coined. That&rsquo;s a funny way to measure the history of blogging, but blogging has a funny history. The original blogs were nothing but simple web pages, the text of which were notes and stories and, most importantly, _links_. Because links were the life of the early web. Before Google, before Twitter and Facebook and Stumbleupon and all our other discovery and filtering tools, things were found by linking to them. People posted entire lists of links online, the cool or fascinating or interesting things they found as they poked around the recesses of the web. It was from there that Google&rsquo;s algorithm was born: the things we link to are the things that matter on the internet. The history of blogs is a bit convoluted; in some ways, they&rsquo;ve come a long way and are no longer recognisable as the things people put online in the nineties. In some ways, blogging hasn&rsquo;t changed at all, and it&rsquo;s starting to show its age.

## Reading is Writing

We want to create a blogging platform that puts links at the center of the web again. Rather than writing a comment on someone else&rsquo;s blog post, you would reply to their blog post on your own blog. Quote them, and link back to the original post. This requires an end to the separation between reading someone else&rsquo;s thoughts and writing your own. You need to be able to consume the blogs you&rsquo;ve subscribed to and reply to them easily. To make this possible, we need to merge the act of consuming the information on the web and contributing new information to the web. Whereas most blogging platforms present a &#34;read view&#34; (the page the post is visible on) and a &#34;write view&#34; (your dashboard, the place you go to write new content), we need to combine those two so that creating content can be done easily from either mode.

Everything is a Stream

As Anil Dash [noted](http://dashes.com/anil/2012/08/stop-publishing-web-pages.html), the battle has been fought and streams have won. We don&rsquo;t want to consume web pages like they are each their own, separate document; we want to consume them as streams, as moments in time, as evolutions and accreted updates. We want to be able to slip into and out of this stream of information, playing the history of that thought backwards or forwards, watching the author evolve as the thought develops. This has been made obvious by the success of microblogging and Tumblr. We want to explore this new paradigm, and align it with the same fundamental properties that made the web what it is today.

Standards or Bust

There are some tools out there that are similar to this&#8212;Tumblr gets a lot of this right, for example&#8212;but all of them are closed-source and fight to keep users locked into their platforms. We want to build a tool, not a platform. We want to embrace RSS and PubsubHubbub. We want to use standards wherever they make sense, and when we create new behaviours or interactions, we want to work to standardise it. Our success will not be measured by the number of users on our platform, it will be measured by the health and sophistication of the blogging landscape.

We Can Do Better

Blogging is an inherently fundamental part of the web as we know it today. Self-publishing is a cornerstone of the web, and that cornerstone deserves tools that keep apace with the developments of the web. If you&rsquo;re interested in our project to build these tools, [sign up for updates about Tangles](http://get.tangl.es). The web can be better, so let&rsquo;s make it better.
